# Brief and narrative review

## Review the argument

Write a one-sentence central line. For each slide record its purpose, claim or question, evidence, message, transition and time. Use the most suitable structure for the topic, such as problem–method–result–implication, question–mechanism–example, or comparison–criteria–decision. Do not force a research argument onto a tutorial or treat a bibliography as a narrative conclusion.

Perform these tests and revise specific failures:

1. **Title-only test:** Read titles in order. Check whether the audience can reconstruct the progression. Titles can be questions or descriptive labels when that serves the talk, but the progression must still be clear.
2. **Because/therefore test:** Explain why each key claim follows from the prior evidence. Identify missing premises, conflicting evidence, causation claims, and limitations; qualify claims or add evidence.
3. **Relevance test:** For each slide identify its purpose and how it advances the central line. Remove, combine, move, or justify material that does not contribute.
4. **Transition test:** State what remains unresolved and why the next slide addresses it. Check prerequisites before conclusions and distinguish a change of topic from a logical consequence.
5. **Audience test:** Identify terms, symbols, assumptions, and context the audience may not know; introduce them before use. Explain what to look at before interpreting a complex figure.
6. **Text flow test:** Read aloud in presentation order. Check that sentences refer clearly to their subjects, line breaks do not mislead, each paragraph develops one idea, and spoken explanation complements rather than merely repeats text. Split or shorten overflow; don't shrink to fit.
7. **Ending test:** Verify that the conclusion answers the opening question, reflects the evidence and uncertainty, and leaves the requested takeaway or next action.
8. **Timing test:** Sum slide estimates, include transitions, demonstrations and the allocated question period, and rehearse against available time when feasible. Flag estimates as estimates; compress or cut low-value material if needed.

Record concrete findings and fixes, not just a checklist of passes. Editorial judgment is required; automation cannot determine whether an argument is persuasive or correct.
